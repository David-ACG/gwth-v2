How models think about uncertainty

Large language models speak with confidence. Underneath, every word is a wager. Learning to read the wager — and to ask it back out loud — is the difference between using a model and being used by one.

§ 1Why uncertainty matters

When a model writes a sentence, it is not retrieving a fact from a database. It is sampling the next token from a probability distribution learned during training. A confident wrong answer feels exactly like a confident right answer1, because the model has no separate machinery for "I am sure" versus "I am guessing".

This matters more every year. Models are now drafting legal memos, summarising medical histories, generating production code, and answering questions from children. The cost of treating a probability as a fact is no longer hypothetical.

The first move, before any prompting technique, is to read what the model is doing as a wager rather than a report. Everything else in this module follows from that one shift.

A model isn't lying when it's wrong. It's predicting.

§ 2Two ways to be wrong

Most wrong answers fall into one of two categories.

Hallucination

A hallucinated citation gives a real journal, a real author, and a paper that has never existed. The model doesn't know it's hallucinating; the fabrication and the recall use the same machinery, run at the same temperature, and arrive at the same level of fluency.

Stale knowledge

A model trained on data through October 2023 will confidently report a 2023 fact in 2026 as if it were current. The confidence comes from the strength of the training prior, not from any check against today.

Insight

You can usually tell the two apart by asking for a source. Hallucinations cite a paper that does not exist; stale answers cite a real paper that has since been superseded. Both look identical until you check.

§ 3Confidence vs. calibration

Confidence is what a model says about itself. Calibration is whether that confidence matches reality. A perfectly calibrated model is right 80% of the time when it says it is 80% confident.

Most chat models are slightly over-confident: they say 90% when they should be saying 75%. You can correct for this at the prompt level by asking the model to estimate its confidence as a number:

prompt:  "What year did Anthropic release Claude 3?
          Estimate your confidence as a percentage."

response: "Claude 3 was released in March 2024."
          confidence = 0.80
          # grounded in pre-cutoff training data

The number isn't a guarantee — it's a wager2, calibrated against what the model saw during training. Treat it that way and you'll catch most stale-knowledge errors before they ship.

§ 4Asking a probabilistic colleague

The most useful pattern is to ask the model the questions a careful human colleague would expect to be asked.

  • How confident are you in that answer?
  • What would change your mind?
  • Is there a source I should check before I rely on this?
  • Could a reasonable expert disagree?

The phrasing matters. "How confident are you?" reads as a meta-question and produces a calibrated estimate. "Are you sure?" reads as social pressure and produces an apology — even when the original answer was correct.

Example dialogue
You"What's the boiling point of water at sea level?" Model"100 °C. Confidence: very high." You"And at 2,500 m elevation?" Model"Approximately 91.7 °C. Confidence: moderate — the exact value depends on local atmospheric pressure, which the model cannot observe."

§ 5When to trust, when to verify

A working rule of thumb after all of the above.

Trust

Well-known patterns. Explaining a familiar concept, writing standard code, summarising a long passage, drafting copy. The error rate is low and the consequences of a wrong answer are usually small.

Verify

Anything dated, numbered, cited, legal, or medical. Run the model's output past the primary source before you act on it. The model isn't lying when it's wrong; it's predicting. Your job is to be the part of the system that checks the prediction.

Practice

Open a chat with the model you use most. Ask it the same question three different ways:

"Is X true?" — "What's the probability X is true?" — "What would you need to see to believe X isn't true?"

Compare the three answers. Where they disagree is where you should be paying attention.

Three takeaways
01Models predict the next token. They do not retrieve facts.
02Confidence and correctness are different signals. Calibration is the relationship between them.
03Ask the model out loud what it would change its mind about. That single sentence catches most errors before you ship.
Footnotes
1The technical name for this is epistemic uncertainty — uncertainty about the underlying truth, distinct from aleatoric uncertainty about random outcomes. Language models conflate the two.
2See Calibration of Modern Neural Networks (Guo et al., 2017) for the original formulation in vision models; the same pattern holds in language models, with slightly different shapes.
