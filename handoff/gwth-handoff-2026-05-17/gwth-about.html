About · GWTH.ai

The patient way to learn AI.

We started GWTH because the conversation around AI was moving faster than most adults could comfortably keep up with — and louder than most adults could comfortably listen to.

Two years ago, you could go a whole week without anyone asking you what you thought about AI. Now the same question turns up in the school gate, the team standup, the dinner conversation. It arrives with the assumption that you've already worked out an answer — and a small ambient suggestion that, if you haven't, you may be in trouble.

The honest position, for most adults, is that they have not. The field moves faster than the courses meant to teach it. The good explanations sit inside long blog posts written by engineers for engineers. The bad explanations sit on TikTok and shout. The bootcamps cost a fortnight you don't have and ten thousand pounds you don't want to spend. And every week the headlines change again.

We thought the problem wasn't a lack of material. It was a lack of pacing.

GWTH is built around the idea that a curious adult, given ten patient minutes a day, can build a serious, current understanding of AI — without ever needing to write a line of code, and without pretending that the field is simpler than it is. We borrowed from the rhythm of a good Sunday paper: a careful column, a small illustration, a pull quote, a place to put your tea down.

The score is honest. The credential is verifiable. The lessons are short on purpose. We do not gamify your streak, we do not sell your data, and we are not running a leaderboard. There's nothing to win except a working understanding of one of the more consequential technologies of your lifetime.

If that's what you'd like to spend an hour on this weekend, you're welcome here.

Six principles

What we will and won't do.

01 · Calm over loud

We use restraint everywhere, including here.

No neon palettes, no urgent banners, no popups, no countdowns. The website is meant to feel like the lessons themselves.

02 · Paper, not plastic

The reader is typeset like editorial, not like SaaS.

Borders and whitespace do the work. Cards have hairline edges. Buttons sit where you'd expect them and disappear when you don't need them.

03 · Honest scores

Your number reflects today, not last February.

The GWTH Score decays as the curriculum updates. You always see your current standing — and so does anyone who verifies your credential.

04 · Real practice

Modules end with a build, not a multiple-choice quiz.

You finish each arc with something you can take to work — a prompt template, a workflow, a small applied piece of writing. The credential reflects what you can do, not what you can recognise.

05 · No vendor capture

We teach concepts, not products.

You'll learn how language models, retrieval, and evaluation actually work — not how to use one specific company's chat box. Skills you take with you, regardless of which provider wins next year.

06 · Quiet variety

Six calm colours, used with intention.

Teal for action. Forest for completion. Amber for things that need attention. Indigo for what's next. Rust only for the brand. Every colour earns its place; nothing decorates for the sake of it.

How a lesson is made

Drafted, reviewed, narrated, refreshed.

Each lesson goes through four stages before a learner sees it. Once published, the date is logged and a quiet clock starts — every lesson is reviewed for currency every six months.

01

Research

An editor and a domain reviewer agree on the lesson's shape, its examples, and the one idea it must leave the reader with.

02

Draft

The lesson is written long, then cut back. Diagrams are drawn last so the writing carries the explanation, not the picture.

03

Review

A second editor reads the lesson cold. A technical reviewer checks every claim. Anything that can't be verified is rewritten or removed.

04

Refresh

Every six months a lesson is re-read by a current expert. When something changes, the lesson is revised — and any score that depended on it is gently nudged down until you've read the update.

A note from the founder

"When I first sat down to learn how language models actually worked, I read three papers, two long blog posts, and watched four YouTube videos. By the end of it I had a working understanding — and also a clear sense that very few adults would be willing to do all of that. The information was available; the patience to assemble it was the missing thing."
