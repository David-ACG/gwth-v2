01

Principles

Six rules the whole system tries to honour. Each component below either obeys them or earns its variance.

P · 01

Warm before contrast.

Off-white paper, not stark white. Deep forest text, not pure black. The page should feel like daylight in a wood-panelled room — never an interrogation.

P · 02

One accent, used twice.

Teal carries action and focus. Rust carries the brand mark and rare emphasis. Everything else recedes so what matters lands.

P · 03

Editorial, not magazine.

Serif headlines for warmth and authority; humanist sans for body and chrome. Read like a thoughtful weekly, not a glossy.

P · 04

Hairlines do the work.

Single-pixel borders, generous whitespace, no shadows. Hierarchy comes from rhythm and weight, not from boxes inside boxes.

P · 05

Numbers are typography.

Scores, deltas, dates — set in tabular serifs or mono. A number on this site is something you can read at a glance and trust.

P · 06

Stillness over motion.

No drifting orbs, no animated gradients, no parallax. Movement is reserved for moments the user caused — a hover, a toggle, a success.

03

Typography · the system

Source Serif 4 carries display and editorial moments. Source Sans 3 carries body, UI, and chrome. Drawn by the same hand, with matched proportions — quiet enough to disappear behind dense content.

Source Serif 4 · Source Sans 3 Display serif · humanist sans · monospace numerics

The serif sets headlines and editorial moments — credentials, pull-quotes, the cover of a lesson. The sans does everything else: paragraphs, tables, form fields, navigation, button labels, captions. Numerics use tabular figures so dashboards line up cleanly without nudging.

Module 04 · Lesson 12

How models think about uncertainty.

Most people assume a language model has a single opinion. It doesn't. At each step it holds a probability distribution over thousands of possible next moves, and what you see in the answer is one path sampled from that distribution.

The practical implication is that asking the same question twice rarely gives you the same answer twice, and that the model's "confidence" is something you can sometimes ask it to expose — but rarely something it volunteers.

Treat the model as a probabilistic colleague, not as an oracle. The right question isn't "is this true?" — it's "how would I check?"

In the next exercise you'll prompt the same question three ways and compare the answers side by side.
