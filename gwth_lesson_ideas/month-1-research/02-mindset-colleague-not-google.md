# AI Is a Colleague, Not a Search Engine — the Month 1 Mental Shift

The single most important lesson in Month 1. Most beginners inherit their AI habits from 25 years of Google: short keywords, one-shot queries, skim links, move on. LLMs *punish* that workflow and reward the opposite — prose, context, role, iteration, conversation. This file is the research spine for L1 and L2.

## The library vs colleague analogy (teach this in L2)

> Google is a **library**. You go in, grab a book, leave.
> AI is a **colleague**. You brief them, they draft, you react, they revise. A **working session**, not a search.

Keep it this simple. The shift lands on its own once students try the same question both ways.

## Four voices that converge on the same answer

### Ethan Mollick (Wharton, *Co-Intelligence*)
The most-cited thinker on human–AI collaboration. Four rules (verbatim from *Co-Intelligence* and his *One Useful Thing* Substack):
1. **Always invite AI to the table.** Try it on every task you do.
2. **Be the human in the loop.** Interrogate the output; do your due diligence.
3. **Treat AI like a person — but tell it what kind of person.** Give it a persona.
4. **Assume this is the worst AI you'll ever use.** Capabilities are still climbing.

His metaphor for beginners: **"an infinitely fast intern, eager to please but prone to bending the truth."** His 2026 escalation: *"Companies should stop thinking of it as a better chatbot and start treating it as a system that can carry out hours of independent work."*

**Jagged frontier.** AI is superhumanly good at some tasks (write a sonnet) and weirdly bad at others (count the words in that sonnet). You only learn the shape of the frontier by *using* it. Teach this in L2 so students stop assuming and start testing.

### Andrej Karpathy (ex-Tesla / OpenAI)
Reframes it for the technical imagination: **"English is the new programming language"** (2023) and **"LLMs are a new kind of computer, and you program them in English"** (Software 3.0 talk, 2025). The liberating implication for a beginner: *you already speak the programming language*. Your job is to learn how to brief a colleague well, not to learn syntax.

He also coined **"vibe coding"** in Feb 2025 — the fully-give-in-to-the-vibes, forget-the-code-exists version that beginners experience in Claude Artifacts and Lovable.

### Daniel Priestley (UK entrepreneur)
Popularises "AI-first business" and "AI as your best employee" to UK audiences through his Substack, LinkedIn, YouTube. The refrain UK students will recognise: *most people use AI as a slightly better Google; the winners use it as a team of employees.*

### NLW / Nathaniel Whittemore (*The AI Daily Brief*)
Consistently frames **2026 as "the Year of the AI Builder"** (see his Lovable CEO interview, Anton Osika). The arc he pushes: prompting tools → delegating to colleagues → commissioning software. His "markets of one" phrase — personal software written for one user, by that user, in an afternoon — captures the Month 1 promise exactly.

## Practical classroom moves

1. **Ban the word "search" in Week 1.** Replace with "ask" or "brief."
2. **Make students do at least one exercise in voice mode** — the conversational interface makes the colleague framing visceral.
3. **Teach persona priming (*"tell it who it is"*) before prompt engineering.** It does more than any technical tweak.
4. **Use Claude Projects or Custom GPTs early** so students experience *continuity* — a colleague who remembers.
5. **Frame the Family AI Bot capstone as "hiring a new household member"**, not "building software." Single reframe, huge motivational lift.

## Five "first AI colleague" exercises (exported as Appendix A of the main doc)

1. **The £5,000 Consultant** — brief AI for 20 min on a problem a consultant would charge £5 k to solve.
2. **The Brutally Honest Self-Review** — ask AI to review *you*, directly, not flatteringly.
3. **The Meta-Prompt Drill** — ask AI to ask *you* five clarifying questions, then write the prompt.
4. **The Jagged Frontier Probe** — give it 5 tasks; find the 2 surprises.
5. **The Iteration Ladder** — iterate one piece 5 times; compare round 1 to round 5.
