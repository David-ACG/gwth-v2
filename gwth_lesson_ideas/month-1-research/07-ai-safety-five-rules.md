# AI Safety — Five Simple Rules (with UK authority)

Beginners need rules, not ethics lectures. Month 1 teaches five rules, each anchored in a UK authority and a UK teaching example. Deep ethics deferred to Month 2/3.

## Rule 1 — Verify

**Why.** AI hallucinates confidently. NCSC: *"LLMs contain serious flaws, including the ability to get things wrong and 'hallucinate' incorrect facts."*

**UK teaching examples (the gold set — all 2025–26):**
- **Ayinde / Al-Haroun** (London High Court, June 2025) — a barrister cited 5 non-existent cases (Ayinde), a solicitor cited 18 hallucinated authorities (Al-Haroun). Court warned of *"severe penalties"*.
- **M v F (Fact Finding Hearing) [2026] EWFC 22** — UK Family Court cross-examined a witness on AI use in their statement.
- **Re A,B,C,D [2026] EWFC 71** — fresh 2026 AI hallucination ruling.
- **Brightwaters Energy v Eroton [2026] EWHC 296** — further 2026 AI hallucination ruling.
- **Layla Parsons** (barrister, March 2026) — self-reported to Bar Standards Board after citing four fabricated cases. *(Law Gazette / ChangeFlow.)*
- Aggregate: **~60 UK hallucination cases** on record by April 2026 (global total 1,100+).

**Teaching habit:** if it matters, check it.

## Rule 2 — Don't share secrets

**Why.** NCSC: *"the query will be visible to the organisation providing the LLM… queries are stored and will almost certainly be used for developing the LLM service."* ICO: if an employee pastes client data into free ChatGPT, **the employer remains the data controller and carries the liability.**

**Rule of thumb:** if you wouldn't post it on LinkedIn, don't paste it into free ChatGPT. Upgrade to paid/Team/Enterprise for anything sensitive.

## Rule 3 — Your stamp

**Why.** AI content needs your review before it goes out with your name on it. You own the tone, claims, mistakes.

**UK teaching example:** **Reform UK AI-generated supporters image (9 April 2026)** — deformed sausage-fingers, nonsensical placard text, used to claim "largest political party by members." Detectors flagged with high confidence. Brand trust damage, fact-checker backlash.

## Rule 4 — Check the date

**Why.** Models have knowledge cutoffs. Prices, laws, policies change.

**UK relevance April 2026:** tax bands, Council Tax, 30-hour childcare, State Pension, FCA rules all move annually — **ask *"when is your data from for this?"*** and verify from a current UK source.

## Rule 5 — Too good to be true

**Why.** Fake citations, hallucinated statistics, invented case law and papers. URLs and titles that look perfect are the highest-risk zone — made-up citations are the #1 hallucination pattern. Copy the URL or title into Google; zero hits → doesn't exist.

**UK teaching example (liar's dividend):** the **RSPCA** had to issue a public statement (10 April 2026) that a viral photo of 250 rescue dogs was **genuine, not AI** — once AI fakes are common, real content gets accused too. Students need to spot *both* directions of error.

## Personal AI Usage Policy template (one-page, what students build)

1. Which tools I'm allowed to use and at what tier (paid vs free).
2. What information I'll never paste into free-tier AI (PII, client data, NHS numbers, passwords).
3. What I always verify before publishing (citations, statistics, quotes, legal/medical/financial claims).
4. What I always disclose (AI-assisted content to my employer/clients where relevant).
5. What I'll do if I catch AI lying (how I correct, retract, remember).

## "Catch the Hallucination" exercise design

- 10 factual claims generated by AI.
- **3 wrong** (classic patterns: fake case law, fake citation, made-up statistic, non-existent person, URL that 404s, wrong date on a famous event).
- **1 real case that *looks* suspicious** — to teach students not to cry wolf.
- Student must identify which, provide a verified source, and note the pattern.
